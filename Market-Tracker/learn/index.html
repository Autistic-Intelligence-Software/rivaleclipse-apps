1Perpetuals in plain English

A perpetual contract (a "perp") is a bet with a clock on it. You pick a crypto, you pick a line — a price — and you pick a cycle: how long until the bet settles (5 minutes, 30 minutes, an hour…). When the cycle closes, only one question matters:

Did the price close OVER your line, or UNDER it?

  • OVER side — you win if the closing price is above your line.
  • UNDER side — you win if the closing price is below your line.
  • Nothing in between counts. A huge move and a one-cent move pay the same if they land on your side.
  • The line is everything: the same price move can be a win or a loss depending on where you set it. Setting the line near the current price makes it a coin flip; setting it far away makes one side likely but cheap, and the other unlikely but expensive.

On real exchanges, perps usually track the price itself (you profit from the size of the move, not just the side) — Lesson 8 shows that version with real numbers. This tracker works in the over/under world: it watches price against your line and estimates the chance of each side, live, as the cycle runs down.

2Reading a chart: the four trend states

Every minute, the tracker sorts each coin into one of four states, using the same maths on every chart below: how far price travelled in the last day compared with how much that coin usually moves (its volatility, σ), whether the move is easing, and how often price crossed its own average. The live tracker runs this on 15-minute, 1-hour and 4-hour windows; the examples here use the 24-hour window with the identical formulas, so the shapes are big enough to see. All four charts are real.

▲ JUMPING — a real climb, not a blip

Price travelled +10.8% in 24 hours — about 2.0× the move this coin typically makes in a day (a ≥0.5σ standardised slope, still accelerating, is what the tracker calls jumping). What to look for: higher highs and higher lows, pullbacks that get bought quickly, most hours closing green.

▼ DROPPING — the mirror image

-4.8% in 24 hours (2.0× the typical daily move, still accelerating down). Lower highs, lower lows, bounces that get sold. When a coin is dropping, OVER needs the fall to stop and reverse before your cycle closes — the trend is not your friend.

≈ RIDING — glued to its average (or to your line)

Price crossed its own 24-hour average 14 times in a day and finished just +0.3% from where it started. On the tracker this shows as RIDING THE AVERAGE — or RIDING THE LINE when price is hugging your line instead. Riding is the hardest state to bet on: tiny wiggles decide the close, so treat any call here as low-confidence unless the cycle is very short.

— LEVELING OUT — the market is asleep

The whole day's range was 0.53% — a fifth of the 2.7% range this coin usually covers in a day. No slope, no energy. Lesson 6 is devoted to this state's cousin, the plateau, because it quietly eats more beginners' money than any crash.

3Waves: how trends are built from legs

Trends rarely move in straight lines. They zig-zag: a push one way (a leg), a pullback, then the next push. String legs together and you get a wave. The cyan line below connects the real turning points (pivots) of a real ten days — a leg counts when price reverses by at least 1.2% (the threshold used for this chart; the tracker draws its live swing stats with a finer one).

What a wave predicts — and what actually happened here

Across this wave, legs averaged ±4.4% over about 15 hours each. The last full leg was -4.8%, so the pattern's call at the end of the window was: next leg up, roughly 4.4% over ~ 15 hours. And this time the wave broke: over the next 48 hours price moved -6.0% — the opposite of the pattern's call. That is the break signal in real life: instead of turning at the pivot, price kept pushing through it. When a leg travels much further than the typical leg size (~4.4% here) without turning, the wave count is dead — do not keep betting on the turn.

  • Use waves for timing, not certainty. A wave says "a turn is due about now, of about this size" — it never says the turn is guaranteed.
  • Counting legs beats watching candles. Three legs in one direction with shrinking size usually means exhaustion; legs that keep matching the rhythm mean the wave is healthy.
  • The break is information too. A leg that blows through the last pivot instead of turning means the pattern changed — the tracker downgrades its confidence exactly then.

4Snipe setups: the squeeze and the shock

Most of the time, sudden moves are luck. But one pattern in our own backtests genuinely beat the base rate, and it is the only one the tracker flags: the squeeze → shock. First, volatility collapses — hours of unusually quiet bars (the squeeze). Under the quiet, one-sided pressure builds: price keeps leaning one way and volume creeps up (the spring loading). Then the coil releases in one fast move (the shock).

The fitted rules (pooled across all 20 coins, hourly bars): squeeze = the last 6 hours' volatility in the bottom 20% of that coin's own history; pressure = the 6-hour move at least 1.0σ one way; shock = a bar beyond 2.5× its typical move on a volume spike. Judged over the following 4 hours, flagged setups hit their big move 23.1% of the time versus a 14.4% base rate — a 1.61× lift. Honest footnote: the same flags did not beat the base rate at the 1-hour horizon or on 15-minute bars. A snipe flag means "elevated chance of a sudden move", never "a move is coming".

A real flagged squeeze, start to finish

  • The squeeze: on flag at 2026-07-24T14:00:00Z, SUI's last-6-hours volatility sat at the 5th percentile of its own history — quieter than 95% of everything it had done before.
  • The pressure: across those quiet hours price still ground -3.5σ down, and volume in the second half of the window ran 4.5× the first half — buyers/sellers were leaning on a coiled spring.
  • The shock: within the next 4 hours price travelled past 1.5× its expected 4-hour volatility in the leaned direction before it managed 0.75× the other way — this instance won the race.

What to look for before it fires: bars getting visibly smaller while price refuses to reverse; volume rising into the quiet; a price that keeps closing near the same edge of its little range. What kills the setup: the quiet ends with a drift back to neutral — pressure gone, no trade.

5Spikes and drops: the 15-minute weather

A spike (or its evil twin, a drop) is one violent bar: here, a 15-minute move of at least 4× the coin's typical bar, on at least double the usual volume. Two real ones, chosen because they teach opposite lessons about warning signs.

▲ Spike — ETH, and the signs WERE there

That bar moved +2.3% in fifteen minutes — a 7.1σ event on 8.7σ volume. Before it fired, the 2-hour volatility was already running at the 78th percentile of its recent history and volume across the approach was trending 3.1× upward. Translation: the market was winding up in plain sight. Spikes like this are what the squeeze lesson (4) hunts in advance.

▼ Drop — XRP, out of a clear sky

-1.7% in fifteen minutes, a 7.6σ bar. And before it? Volatility at the 2nd percentile — nearly the quietest stretch in its recent history — and volume trending 0.8×, i.e. flat. No squeeze, no build-up, no warning in the price or volume data this system tracks. Some drops are news, liquidations, or one huge seller, and no chart pattern sees them coming. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

The tradable difference: you cannot predict the second kind. You can only size for it — never stake so much on one cycle that a 5–8σ bar against you ends your day.

6Plateaus: the profit killer

A plateau is a stretch where price goes nowhere, slowly. No trend pays you, no wave completes, and every over/under close is decided by noise — while fees, spread and your own impatience take a cut of every attempt. It does not feel dangerous. That is exactly why it is.

Three full days of BCH: total range 2.0%, net move 0.01%. A normal 72 hours for this coin covers about 8.5% — this stretch managed a quarter of that. Every line you could have set inside that band would have been crossed dozens of times by pure jitter.

  • Why the tracker marks these red: on the advisory board, a compressed range against the coin's own volatility is the plateau signal — red means "likely to bleed", not "about to move". A plateau ending is a different pattern (that's the squeeze, Lesson 4); a plateau continuing is the base case.
  • The correct move is usually no trade. Sitting out costs nothing. The second-best move is a much shorter cycle, where at least the noise resolves quickly.
  • Never "make it back" inside a plateau. Doubling stakes in a range that has already eaten two bets is how quiet markets take loud money.

7Using this tracker

Everything in these lessons is wired into the tracker itself. What you are looking at on a coin card:

  • The call and the line. Each card shows the system's suggested side — OVER or UNDER — against your line for the current cycle, scored only on the prediction locked in when the cycle opened. The live number can move; the grade never rewrites history.
  • NO EDGE is a real answer. When the estimated probability sits inside the system's uncertainty band, the card says NO EDGE instead of dressing up a coin flip. Most of the time, on most coins, that is the honest answer — a tracker that always has a pick is a tracker that is lying to you on schedule.
  • ● Green — best opportunity. Trend clarity, tradable volatility, and often a wave or snipe structure lining up. Green coins are ranked; the top strip names the single most predictable coin right now.
  • ● Red — plateau / unpredictable. Compressed range, flip-flopping direction, or weak evidence. Red does not mean "bet against it" — it means the system expects this market to bleed anyone who trades it.
  • The forecast check. Every 30 minutes the system draws its own predicted price path for each coin — drift plus the expected swing pattern — freezes it, and then grades itself against what price actually did at 5, 10, 15 and 30 minutes. The record sits in each card's Details section. It is the same method Lesson 3 teaches, run in public, on itself.
  • The global scoreboard. The header shows the system's whole record — every visitor sees the same numbers, pulled from the one shared database. Nothing is scored on your device, nothing is uploaded by your visit, and there is no account to log into.
How to use all of it together: check the scoreboard (is the system even earning trust today?), prefer green coins, respect NO EDGE, size for the Lesson 5 bar you cannot see coming, and let plateaus stay red and untraded.

8Position craft: enter, add, cash out, flip

Getting the direction right is half the trade. The other half is what you do while the position is open. On an exchange, a perpetual position is not a lottery ticket you hold to the end — you can add to it, trim it, close it early, or reverse it, and each choice reprices your risk instantly. This worked example uses a real move and real prices throughout; stakes are a flat $1,000 per step so the arithmetic is easy to audit. Fees and funding are ignored (they would shave every number slightly). The tracker advises; it does not place trades — these buttons exist on your exchange, not here.

1 · Enter, then the first leg

$1,000 long at 0.00000244. The first leg ran +15.6% before the pullback — at the add point the position was already up +$127.05.

2 · Add on confirmation

The dip was -2.1% and held — so the second $1,000 went in at 0.00000275. Average cost 0.00000259. Adding after confirmation, not before, is the whole trick.

3 · Cash out early (half)

At the peak, 0.00000284, selling half banked +$98.33 of locked profit. Selling everything there was worth +$196.66 in total — more, but only hindsight knows it was the peak. Half is the compromise: paid, and still in the game.

4 · Flip when it breaks

The close below the pullback low killed the long story. Closing the rest banked +$55.79 more; the $1,000 short then earned +$18.32 as price slid to 0.00000268. Doing nothing at the flip and holding the long to the end would have finished at +$72.91 instead of +$172.44.

The scoreboard for this one move

Read the gaps honestly: the plan beat holding because of the flip, not the entry — the entry alone was worth little once the trend broke. And the hindsight row beats the plan, which is the standing tax on every real decision: you act with the information at the time, not the information in the chart afterwards. Fees, funding and slippage would narrow every gap above; a faster reversal would have punished the flip. Position craft is risk management, not a money printer.
